Norristown grew where the Schuylkill River, canals and mills met, and its older cores still hold twins, rowhouses and subdivided larger homes from that era. A remodel or home purchase there should start by learning which painted surfaces contain lead.

A county seat built on river power

The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia describes how the Schuylkill and its tributaries powered mills and carried goods to Philadelphia, turning Norriton into the borough that became the Montgomery County seat.

Growth came in waves, so one block can hold 1880s housing while the next is postwar. Additions and renovations can hide original painted components, and the age of the neighborhood does not answer the age of every surface.

What that means for lead

Norristown's older cores mix twins, rowhouses and subdivided larger homes. A renovation plan should start by identifying which painted surfaces the work will actually touch, room by room.

For a Norristown project, separate the original house from later additions in your notes. A newer kitchen does not make the original rooms lead-free. Ask for a report that names tested components clearly enough to guide the contractor who does the next phase.

Common questions

I am buying a 1920s Norristown twin. Is a general home inspection enough?

A general inspection does not identify lead paint surface by surface. A lead-based paint inspection uses XRF or lab analysis and gives you a room-by-room record before you remodel.

The house was remodeled once already. Does that remove the risk?

Not by itself. New finishes can cover lead paint without removing it. Ask what the earlier work disturbed, and test the surfaces your project will touch.
